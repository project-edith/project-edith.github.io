Motivation

Human intent is not expressed solely through language

A language-conditioned policy acts only on what is stated explicitly, so the human has to put their entire intent into words. EDITH instead conditions on the human's first-person view and eye gaze together with their speech.

System

Hardware system: smart glasses capture the user's
egocentric context for the robot to act on

We build a hardware system in which the user's egocentric context — first-person view, speech, and eye gaze — is captured by smart glasses and aligned with the robot's observations in real time.

View · Gaze · Speech Real-time synchronisation

Why the view and the gaze, and not just the voice?

The view says what the human is doing. The gaze says which part of it they care about right now. Between them they carry the half of the request that never makes it into words.

The glasses are Project Aria. Their camera, eye tracker, and microphone stream to the robot's server, the speech is transcribed as it arrives, and every frame is timestamped against the robot's own camera.

Using the framework yourself

While our experiments focus on learning policies that follow pointing and gaze-based referring instructions, the hardware framework itself is task-agnostic, and our codebase supports both data collection and real-robot inference. It can be used for a range of human-robot interactive and collaborative settings, for example:

  • Collaborative assembly. The human keeps both hands on the workpiece and asks for the next part or tool just by looking at it, while the robot stages and hands over the right one.
  • Proactive assistance. Gaze and the first-person view arrive before the request does, so the robot can anticipate what is needed next instead of waiting to be asked.
  • Modelling human behaviour. The time-aligned egocentric and robot streams form a corpus for studying how people refer to things — when gaze leads speech, and when a gesture alone is enough.

Method

Following referring instructions with a hierarchical policy

We design a policy that follows referring instructions grounded in nonverbal signals such as pointing and eye gaze. Since these signals are rich but noisy, EDITH uses a hierarchical policy: the high-level policy reads the egocentric stream and extracts keyframe-grounded subtasks, and the low-level policy conditions on those keyframes to produce actions. The same hierarchy runs on every task — switch tasks to see it resolve a different request.

    The language-only baselines barely get off the ground: \(\pi_l^{\mathrm{lang}}\) and \(\pi_h^{\mathrm{lang}} + \pi_l^{\mathrm{lang}}\) average 4.2% and 6.9% success. Without the egocentric stream there is nothing in the instruction that identifies the target.

    Conditioning an end-to-end policy directly on the current egocentric context is not enough either — \(\pi_l^{\mathrm{ego+lang}}\) reaches 6.2%. It helps only while gaze stays on the target and degrades as soon as attention becomes intermittent.

    \(\pi_{\mathrm{FAM\text{-}HRI}} + \pi_l\), which infers intent from gaze and speech before acting, is the strongest baseline at 25.0% SR and 57.1% TP. EDITH roughly doubles that success rate by monitoring intent separately in the high-level policy and handing the low-level policy a keyframe-grounded subtask.

    Robustness

    EDITH is unaffected when the human is distracted

    Mid-task, the human turns away to something else — checking a phone while still instructing. The baseline here is \(\pi_{\mathrm{FAM\text{-}HRI}} + \pi_l\), which reads the same gaze and speech, and its success rate nearly halves. EDITH's does not move.

    Ablations

    Ablations on the subtask representation

    A subtask is an instruction paired with a keyframe taken from the human's own view. We ablate each of the two components on Muffin-Serving.

    Effect of the keyframe

    EDITH w/o keyframe EDITH

    Without the keyframe, the low-level policy receives only the instruction. Success on Muffin-Serving drops from 43.8% to 2.1%: the policy knows what kind of object to pick up, but not which one.

    Effect of the egocentric viewpoint

    EDITH w/o ego-view EDITH

    Replacing the egocentric keyframe with one taken from the robot's own camera drops success to 2.1% as well. The information is present in the scene, but not available from that viewpoint.

    Why the keyframe has to come from the human's view

    The robot can see the same table, the same muffins, and the same hand. What it cannot see is the line between them. A pointing gesture only resolves along the axis the pointer is looking down — seen from the side, the finger hovers between several candidates at once. That is parallax, and it is why the keyframe is captured where the intent is formed rather than where the arm happens to be.

    Why are human signals noisy?

    Eye gaze can shift unstably frame by frame, and the egocentric view changes frequently as the human moves. These temporal changes make the signal rich but noisy.
